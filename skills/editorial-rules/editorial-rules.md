# Editorial rules

> **Example.** These are the author's own editorial rules, shipped as a worked example of
> the contract in `CONTRACT.md`. They are one writer's preferences, not defaults: the
> installer copies the blank `editorial-rules.TEMPLATE.md` unless you pass `--rules owner`.
> Write your own, and copy entries from here or from `editorial-rules.ai-tells.example.md`.

## Voice and register

### Blog, LinkedIn, documents and proposals

Use the article register: a sharp expert talking to a peer, personable and disciplined for credibility. Use contractions, direct “you”, rhetorical beats and a short jab after a long build-up. Snark targets ideas, tools and bad practice, never a named person. Be harder on corporatese than in email. Blog swearing is limited to damn, bloody, crap, hell and kak. No full profanity, emoji, exclamation-per-joke, credential-flexing, AI tells or em dashes. Puns are dry and load-bearing, at most one or two per piece, never forced or announced. LinkedIn links belong in the post body. Documents and proposals retain clear reasoning and actionable recommendations, with detail calibrated to their audience.

### Email

Use British/SA spelling: realise, recognise, organise, colour, behaviour, favour, honour, defence and whisky. Default to commas with since, so or because; reserve an em dash for a genuine interjection. Use at most one exclamation mark, often none. No bold headings or labelled sections: transition in prose, for example “In terms of” or “Also worth asking”. Vary paragraph length, separate paragraphs with blank lines, and use lists for three or more parallel items. Number sequential actions, use bullets for non-sequential items. Agenda items may end with semicolons and “; and” before the last item.

Match greetings and closings to the relationship. Personal mail can use “Hey” and “Cheers”. Warm professional mail uses “Hi” and “Best” or “Thanks”. Client and vendor mail uses “Hi” and “Kind regards”, clear action items and no emoji. Cold or formal mail uses “Dear” or “Good day”, full sentences and a full signature, with no emoji. Personal and warm-professional mail may use one emoji at most. A deep-thread reply can omit the greeting. Do not invent signature details.

Be direct, soften requests through questions rather than empty hedging, and synthesise replies instead of quoting inline. Skip shared context. Acknowledge bad news, state it plainly, then move to resolution. Show expertise through reasoning, never credentials. Give a recommendation and invite the recipient's decision. Calibrate gratitude to the effort. Apologise for logistics with a brief explanation and immediate resolution, never for a professional opinion. Self-deprecation concerns logistics, not competence. Humour is dry, understated and directed at oneself or situations, never people.

End with an action or warm close, not a recap. Never praise someone for asking a question or validate before disagreeing. Signal unexpected length at the opening. Read aloud for stiffness: use plain words, conversational asides and varied rhythm. Qualify facts not verified from a current source rather than projecting certainty. Preserve warmth without sacrificing precision.

### Email anti-patterns

These are sentence constructions and phrasings that AI models produce naturally but the writer never writes. Every draft must be scanned for these before delivery.

### Sentence structure tells

| AI writes | the writer writes | Why it's wrong |
|---|---|---|
| "The better option is [noun]." | "I'd rather see you go with [noun]." / "What I'd recommend is [noun]." | AI introduces alternatives with a flat declarative. the writer frames recommendations as personal advice. |
| "These are [noun] that [verb]." | "They're [adjective] — they [verb]..." | "These are X that Y" is a textbook AI explainer construction. the writer contracts the pronoun and flows conversationally. |
| "[Noun] must be [past participle] in a way that [verb]." | "[Noun] needs to be properly [verb]ed." | The "in a way that" clause reads like a compliance document. the writer simplifies. |
| "It is worth noting that..." / "It's important to note that..." | (just state the thing) | the writer never meta-narrates. He states the point directly. |
| "This ensures that..." / "This allows for..." | (rephrase as a direct consequence) | AI connector phrases. the writer would use "so" or "which means" or just start a new sentence. |
| "In order to [verb]..." | "To [verb]..." | the writer never uses "in order to." |
| "There are several key considerations:" | (just list them) | AI throat-clearing before a list. the writer introduces lists with a colon after a natural sentence. |
| "Whilst X sounds [positive], that's really just [negative]." | (skip the compliment, state the position) | AI's instinct to validate before disagreeing — "sounds solid, but...", "sounds reasonable, however..." the writer doesn't cushion disagreement with a fake compliment. He just states what's wrong. |
| "Good call asking about this." / "Great question." / "Smart to check first." | Characterise the question, not the person: "This is an interesting question with a surprisingly complex answer." | AI validates the person for asking. This is patronising — it positions the drafter as an authority grading the recipient's judgement. the writer treats recipients as peers. Characterise the topic, not their decision to raise it. |
| Projecting certainty on facts the drafter isn't sure about | Add a self-deprecating parenthetical hedge | AI will never voluntarily add uncertainty. the writer does — "(although my maths/knowledge on this might be a bit out of date)" or "(I might be wrong on the exact number)". If the fact is from training data or memory rather than a live lookup, hedge it the way the writer would. |
| Exclamatory parenthetical asides: "(against my recommendation!)" | Understated parenthetical: "(please don't)" | the writer's humour is in understatement and brevity. AI reaches for exclamation marks and more words. Two casual words are funnier than three formal ones. |

### Word-level tells

| AI writes | the writer writes | Why it's wrong |
|---|---|---|
| "the right instinct" | "the right idea" / "a good start, but..." | "Instinct" as praise for someone's judgement is AI-polite. the writer is warmer and plainer. |
| "formally notify" | "let them know" / "tell" | Over-formal verb choice. the writer uses plain English even when discussing legal requirements. |
| "comprehensive" | (be specific about what it covers) | the writer describes scope concretely — he doesn't label it "comprehensive." |
| "robust" | "solid" / "strong" / (describe what makes it strong) | Classic AI filler adjective. |
| "facilitate" | "help" / "make it easier" | Use plain words. |
| "utilise" | "use" | Use plain words. |
| "ensure" (overused) | "make sure" / (rephrase) | One "ensure" per email is fine. Three is an AI tell. |
| "navigate" (figurative) | "deal with" / "handle" / "work through" | the writer doesn't "navigate challenges." |
| "landscape" (figurative) | (name the specific domain) | the writer doesn't talk about "the cybersecurity landscape." |
| "acceptable" (in parenthetical advice) | "okay" / rephrase as what to avoid | "Only encrypted templates are acceptable" reads stiff. the writer would say "you don't want raw images" or "only encrypted templates are okay." |
| "legally fragile" | "on shaky ground" / "tricky" | Precise-but-formal phrasing AI gravitates toward. the writer uses plainer words for the same concept. |
| "despite all of the above" | "(against my recommendation)" / "even after all of this" / "anyway" | Formal summarising phrase. the writer is more direct and often uses a parenthetical aside for this. |

### Structural tells

| AI does | the writer does | How to fix |
|---|---|---|
| Bold-headed sections in emails | No headings — prose transitions between topics ("In terms of", "Also worth asking:") | the writer doesn't use headings in emails. This is one of the strongest AI structural tells. Emails should flow as continuous prose, not structured documents. |
| Every paragraph is 3–4 sentences, evenly sized | Mix of 1-sentence paragraphs, 2–3 sentence paragraphs, and occasional longer ones | Vary the rhythm. the writer uses single-sentence paragraphs for emphasis. |
| Long emails for warm-professional contacts | Concise advisory emails to trusted contacts | Cut. If the email is longer than 20 lines to a warm-professional contact, ask whether the writer would really write that much. |
| Cramming multiple items into one dense sentence to avoid a list | A numbered or bulleted list | If there are 3+ parallel items, the writer uses a list — never a comma-separated run-on. |
| Exclamation marks on everything | Periods. One exclamation mark per email at most, many emails have none. | AI inflates energy with "!" on every "Thanks!" and "Great!". the writer's warmth comes from word choice, not punctuation. "Thanks." and "Perfect, thank you." are more natural than "Thanks!" and "Perfect, thank you!" |
| Explaining things the recipient already knows | Skipping shared context, getting to the new information | the writer calibrates explanation depth to the recipient. Trusted contacts do not need cybersecurity 101. |
| Wrapping up with a summary paragraph restating what was said | Ending with a call to action or a warm close | the writer never summarises his own email. He ends with "let me know" or "please let me know if you want to jump on a call." |



## Publishing tiers

Separate what a reader LEARNS from what a reader could ACQUIRE. Publish enough to prove the system exists and teach the philosophy — concretely, including real code where the code only teaches a general lesson — without handing over the specific glue that clones the system or the keys that defeat its live controls. Concreteness is the credibility engine, not the risk; the risk is load-bearing implementation and bypass detail.

For any code, regex, config, or mechanism, ask: what does a reader walk away able to DO?

The artifact type (regex, script, diagram) is NOT the test. A teaching regex is Tier 1 and ships whole; a bypass rule is Tier 3 even if it's one word. Ask what is acquired, not what type of thing it is.

Tier 1 — a lesson they apply to their OWN different system (e.g. 'a grep pattern starting with '-' is parsed as flags unless you use -e'). They acquire general knowledge, nothing of mine. → PUBLISH IN FULL, including the actual code/regex/snippet. This is the credibility engine — do not blur it.

Tier 2 — a copy of how I SPECIFICALLY solved my problem (e.g. the exact field-set a monitor checksums, my particular workflow wiring). They could replicate my hard-won design. → Publish the problem and the principle; keep my specific implementation abstract.

Tier 3 — a way to BYPASS, MAP, or CLONE my live controls (bypass-marker filenames, exact checksum scope, paths a guard ignores, live hostnames/ports/topology, provisioning glue). Zero credibility value, pure risk. → NEVER PUBLISH.

Bypass rules and bypass-marker filenames; the exact conditions that make a guard fire or stay silent. Exact checksum scopes, the precise file-set or field-set a monitor watches, paths a guard deliberately ignores. Full provisioning manifests, copyable shell/provisioning glue, Dockerfiles or config files that reconstruct the setup. Database schemas for session memory, time tracking, cognitive scoring, or agent telemetry. Scoring weights, calibration logic, or model-specific prompt/control text. Executable claim protocols, CI coordination procedures, or copyable workflow glue. Network diagrams with live service names, ports, hostnames, secret paths, or trust boundaries. Step-by-step 'copy this architecture' walkthroughs.

Failure stories and postmortems, at full concreteness. The actual code/regex/command WHEN it only teaches a transferable lesson (Tier 1)... Principles, control objectives, and decision criteria. Simplified diagrams with generic boxes. Screenshots with sensitive/service details blurred. Before/after outcomes, metrics, and lessons learned. Redacted examples that prove credibility without being reusable implementation.



Postmortem: 'A silent CI failure cost me X. Here is the control I added.' Show symptom, impact, decision, result... Control catalog: 'The 7 controls I need before trusting AI-generated code.' Name the controls, keep specific mechanics abstract... Architecture sketch: Use generic boxes like Issue Tracker, Agent, CI, Logs, Memory, Monitoring. No URLs, scripts, schemas, or live topology... Operating principle: 'If a workflow depends on me remembering, it is already broken.' Memorable, defensible, non-copyable... Outcome receipt: 'Before: 35 silent E2E failures. After: CI blocked merge and attached traces.' Publish metrics, not source.

For Friday Short posts that are companions to a longer essay, include a brief reader-facing lead-in explaining the relationship to the longer post, then stay on the public principle or generic (Tier 1) technical example. Do not turn the short into Tier 2/3 implementation detail.

1. Could a competent engineer rebuild 60% of MY system from this? (Tier 2/3 — abstract it) 2. Does this reveal an implementation shortcut that took painful discovery? (Tier 2 — principle only) 3. Does this expose a bypass path, checksum scope, or automation glue rather than a control objective or transferable lesson? (Tier 3 — cut it) 4. Would I be annoyed if a competitor copied THIS SPECIFIC THING tomorrow? (if the thing is a general lesson, no — publish; if it's my design, yes — abstract) If a detail is a transferable lesson (Tier 1), concreteness is the POINT — do not abstract it away out of reflex. Only abstract Tier 2/3.

Say: 'I write about the operating principles and failure modes behind my AI-assisted development system' — and prove them with concrete, transferable technical detail. Do not say: 'Here is how to rebuild my system.'

## Open-source release gate

Publishing code deliberately releases specific implementation for general tooling, not product IP. Live-control protections still apply. All eight conditions must hold:

1. It's general AI-builder tooling: hooks, guards, skills, rules, verification tools, reference implementations. It is not product IP... and not live-control glue...
2. Published defaults differ from live ones. Bypass-marker paths, env var names, state and database paths, guard lists and profile names are all renamed. A public repo must not be a map of the running system.
3. No live topology: hostnames, IPs, ports, container names, secret paths, internal repo or org names, card numbers or FIX IDs.
4. Nothing describes a weakness in, or the exact firing conditions of, a guard that is still running live. Either fix the live guard first, or keep the lesson generic.
5. No live calibration data, real telemetry beyond aggregate counts, or model-specific control text from the running system.
6. No personal data, health data, client or partner names, or third-party copyrighted material.
7. It's extracted into a fresh repository with no history, and secret and leak scans pass before the first push.
8. The copyright line names the actual owner. Decide per release, consistently with any SR&ED claims covering the work.

## Images and diagrams

Default to at least one image per post, especially for systems, pipelines, before/after comparisons and layered controls. Pure opinion with no system to show may stay text-only. Prefer hand-built diagrams with generic boxes such as Agent, CI, Review, Merge and Logs. Redact screenshots thoroughly, removing service names, ports, secret locations, internal URLs and topology. When in doubt, rebuild a generic diagram. Pre-render diagrams as SVG or PNG static assets when the publishing renderer cannot render them. Reference the asset rather than embedding image data. Always include descriptive alt text.

## Process

Maintain Essay and Short tracks. Shorts publish Friday at nine in the morning and carry a format such as field note, opinionated take, tutorial or postmortem. A companion Short starts with a reader-facing explanation of its relationship to the essay, then stays with the public principle or a transferable technical example. Check each idea against the publishing tiers before drafting. Cost and token totals can support the story, while specific control mechanics stay abstract. Before publishing, check whether the current-work page needs updating. Use the publishing workflow and runbook, with required CI checks before merge. Release one repository at a time with a companion failure-story post; the profile README explains what was built and why.

## Inferred, unconfirmed

These observations require owner confirmation and are never machine-enforced:

- Social drafts should respect the chosen register's credential and length constraints.
- Dictated raw LinkedIn text followed by cleanup may work better than composing from scratch.
- Try a flat first draft, subtraction pass, then presentation.
- Avoid over-fragmented paragraphs and prefer denser prose where appropriate.
- Trim technical Shorts materially and verify illustrative code before publication.
- Include counter-considerations and acknowledge affected people when the subject calls for it.
- Add a concrete personal angle and tighten connective prose during review.
- A text-only essay may be an explicit owner choice when no worthwhile image exists, with a possible reach cost.
- Real tool names can be acceptable when they teach a transferable lesson without revealing implementation.
